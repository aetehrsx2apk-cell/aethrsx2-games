1. Introduction to AetherSX2

AetherSX2 showed up at a strange time. PCSX2 had been the go-to PS2 emulator on desktop for years, and mobile PS2 emulation was mostly a mess of half-finished projects that crashed more than they ran. Then AetherSX2 landed on Android and, almost overnight, people were booting up games like God of War II on phones that cost less than a AAA game release.

What makes it worth talking about isn't just that it works — plenty of emulators technically "work." It's that AetherSX2 manages to run PS2 titles at playable speeds on hardware that has no business handling PlayStation 2 architecture. The developer built it around the same core as PCSX2, which already had over a decade of compatibility fixes behind it, so a lot of the heavy lifting was already done before it ever touched a phone screen.

I'll be upfront: it's not perfect. Some games stutter, some need very specific settings, and battery drain is real. But if you grew up with a PS2 and haven't touched your old console in years, this is probably the easiest way back into that library without digging a dusty console out of a closet.

2. Why PS2 Games Still Feel Fun Today

It's easy to assume older games only appeal to nostalgia, but that's not really what's happening when people go back to the PS2 era. A lot of these games were built during a period when developers were still figuring out 3D design, and that experimentation produced some genuinely strange, ambitious ideas that modern games rarely attempt anymore.

Take something like Shadow of the Colossus. There's no filler, no side quests, no crafting menu. Just you, a horse, and sixteen giant creatures. Modern open-world games could learn a thing or two from how confidently that game says no to padding.

There's also a simplicity to PS2-era controls and menus that feels almost relaxing now. You're not managing a skill tree with forty branches or juggling five currencies. You pick up the controller, and within a minute you're playing the actual game instead of sitting through tutorials.

And honestly, a lot of it comes down to how these games sound and feel. The music, the slightly rough textures, the way characters move — it's a specific era of design that hasn't been replicated since, mostly because the industry moved on to different priorities. Playing them again on AetherSX2 doesn't feel like time travel exactly, but it does feel like visiting a friend you haven't seen in a while.

3. Top PS2 Games You Can Play Using AetherSX2

This isn't a random top-ten pulled from a wiki. These are games I've personally loaded up, tested for at least an hour or two, and would actually recommend to someone starting out with the emulator. Performance can vary depending on your phone, but this is roughly what to expect on a mid-range Android device from the last couple of years.

God of War II

ActionStory-driven

Kratos hunting down the Fates across a mythological Greece that somehow still looks impressive despite its age. The camera work and set-piece battles hold up surprisingly well.

People keep coming back to it because the combat feels weighty without being overly complicated, and the boss fights still rank among the best in the genre.

Emulation experience: Runs close to full speed on most mid-range phones once shaders are configured correctly. Cutscenes are smooth; combat-heavy areas can dip occasionally.

Tip: Enable the software renderer briefly during the opening Colossus of Rhodes sequence if you notice graphical glitches — it clears up almost every visual bug in that scene.

Shadow of the Colossus

AdventureMinimalist

A quiet, empty world built entirely around sixteen boss encounters. There's barely a story told through dialogue — most of it comes through atmosphere.

It's still referenced constantly in game design discussions because of how much it accomplishes with so little hand-holding.

Emulation experience: This one's tricky. Frame pacing was inconsistent even on the original hardware, and some emulator builds exaggerate that. Expect occasional frame drops on open plains.

Tip: Cap the frame limiter manually instead of using auto-detect — it smooths out the horseback riding sections noticeably.

Need for Speed: Most Wanted

RacingOpen World

Cop chases, a genuinely memorable soundtrack, and car customization that felt ahead of its time. The Blacklist rival system still gives racing games a run for their money.

People revisit it for the chase intensity — few racing games since have made police pursuits feel this tense.

Emulation experience: One of the smoother titles on this list. Runs at near-full speed with minimal tweaking required.

Tip: Turn on widescreen patching in the game properties menu for a noticeably better view during high-speed chases.

GTA San Andreas

Open WorldSandbox

CJ returning to Los Santos is basically a rite of passage for anyone who grew up gaming in the 2000s. The map size alone was unheard of at the time.

Its comedy, radio stations, and sheer amount of things to do keep pulling people back even decades later.

Emulation experience: Very playable, though draw distance settings matter a lot here since the game world is large and detailed for its era.

Tip: Lower the internal resolution slightly if you notice pop-in while driving fast — it trades a bit of sharpness for smoother streaming.

Resident Evil 4

Survival HorrorAction

The game that reshaped third-person shooters. Leon's trip through a village full of infected villagers is tense from the first ten minutes onward.

Its pacing and enemy design are still studied by developers, which says a lot about how far ahead it was.

Emulation experience: Runs great on almost any modern Android phone. This is often recommended as a first game to try on AetherSX2.

Tip: Stick to the default renderer here — some custom shader combos actually make lighting look worse than it should.

Tekken 5

FightingMultiplayer

Arguably the most complete Tekken package on PS2, bundling in earlier titles alongside a roster that still feels balanced today.

Fighting game communities still bring it up because of how tight the controls feel compared to some later entries.

Emulation experience: Extremely stable. Fighting games generally emulate well since they don't demand much from the hardware.

Tip: Use a physical Bluetooth controller if you can — touch controls make frame-precise inputs much harder.

Burnout 3: Takedown

Pure chaos in the best way. Crashes are the reward here, not the punishment, and the speed sense is still unmatched by most modern racers.

It's the kind of game people mention whenever the topic of "most fun racing game ever" comes up.

A jungle-set espionage story with camouflage mechanics and boss fights unlike anything else from that generation.

Its narrative depth and character writing keep it near the top of "best PS2 games" conversations even now.

The largest roster the series ever had on PS2, with destructible arenas and over-the-top special moves that fans still consider the series peak.

It gets replayed constantly because no later DBZ game matched its sheer content volume.

5. Tips for Low-End Android Devices

Not everyone's running a flagship phone, and that's fine — a lot of PS2 games were never that demanding to begin with. Here's what actually helps on budget hardware, based on testing with a couple of older mid-range devices.

  • Close background apps before launching. PS2 emulation is CPU-heavy, and background processes eat into that budget fast.
  • Stick with native resolution. It's tempting to bump this up for sharper visuals, but on weaker chips it's usually the first thing causing stutter.
  • Disable extra shader effects. Things like ambient occlusion or texture filtering look nice but cost more than they're worth on low-end GPUs.
  • Use game-specific fixes when available. Some titles have known compatibility quirks, and checking a PS2 emulator guide before diving in can save an hour of frustrated troubleshooting.
  • Keep your phone cool. Thermal throttling is real — a phone case or fan attachment can meaningfully help sustained performance during longer sessions.

6. Common AetherSX2 Problems and Fixes

Game freezes on the loading screen

Usually tied to a bad BIOS dump or corrupted ISO. Re-check that your BIOS file is unmodified and try re-dumping or re-downloading your game file from a clean source.

Audio crackling or popping

Try switching the audio backend or increasing latency slightly. This trades a tiny bit of input delay for much cleaner sound output.

Graphical glitches in specific scenes

Some games have known rendering quirks tied to certain renderers. Switching between Vulkan and OpenGL temporarily often resolves scene-specific bugs.

Save files not loading correctly

Make sure you're not switching memory card slots between sessions accidentally, and back up saves periodically to avoid losing progress from an unexpected corruption.

Phone overheating quickly

Lower your resolution scaling and close unused background apps. Extended emulation sessions push a phone's CPU harder than most everyday apps.

7. FAQ Section

Is AetherSX2 legal to use?

The emulator itself is legal software. Whether the games you load onto it are legal depends on how you obtained them — generally, dumping your own physical copies is the safest approach.

Do I need a powerful phone to run PS2 games?

Not necessarily. Many titles run fine on mid-range hardware from the past few years. Demanding, open-world games will need more processing power than smaller fighting or sports titles.

Can I use a controller instead of touch controls?

Yes, and it's honestly recommended for anything involving precise timing, like fighting games or action titles with combo systems.

Why does one game run better than another on the same phone?

PS2 games vary wildly in how they use the console's hardware. Some rely more on CPU work, others push graphical effects harder, so performance really is game-specific.

Is there a single best setting for every game?

Not really — that's part of why checking an AetherSX2 compatibility resource for a specific title can save time compared to guessing through trial and error.

8. Final Thoughts

Going back through this list reminded me why the PS2 era gets talked about so fondly. It wasn't just about graphics or technical achievement — a lot of these games took creative risks that bigger budgets today rarely allow. Playing them again on a phone doesn't erase the extra button prompts or the occasional dip in frame rate, but it does prove these games were built on ideas solid enough to survive two decades of gaming trends.

If you're picking a starting point, I'd say begin with something forgiving like Resident Evil 4 or Tekken 5 before jumping into trickier titles like Shadow of the Colossus. Get comfortable with your settings first, then work your way through the rest of the list at your own pace. There's no rush — these games have already waited twenty years, a few more evenings of fine-tuning won't hurt.
